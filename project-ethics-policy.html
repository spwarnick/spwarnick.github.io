Overview

Course Overview

Cybersecurity does not exist in a vacuum — it intersects with law, ethics, policy, and social impact in ways that shape how practitioners operate at every level. This graduate course examined the legal and ethical dimensions of cybersecurity through case law analysis, policy writing, ethical frameworks, and applied social entrepreneurship. The assignments below represent a cross-section of that work, demonstrating the ability to analyze legal precedent, reason through ethical dilemmas, and think strategically about cybersecurity's role in society.

Social Entrepreneurship

Building Digital Resilience: Community Cybersecurity Awareness for All

Proposal

Developed a full grant proposal for a community cybersecurity awareness program in response to a hypothetical $35,000 seed grant from the Green Wolverine Enterprise Group. The proposal identified a gap in accessible, community-level cybersecurity education — a market dominated by costly enterprise-focused training products — and designed a grassroots solution to address it.

The program, offered in a "pay what you can" tiered pricing model, would deliver beginner, intermediate, and advanced cybersecurity training to individuals, families, and small businesses through in-person and virtual formats. The proposal included a phased implementation timeline across three stages — planning, pilot, and full launch — a fully itemized $35,000 budget, a stakeholder communication plan, and a risk register with mitigation strategies.

Case Law — Contract Law

FIRAC Brief: ProCD, Inc. v. Zeidenberg (7th Cir. 1996)

FIRAC Brief

One of the foundational cases in cyberspace contract law, ProCD v. Zeidenberg established that shrinkwrap licenses — terms revealed only after purchase — are legally enforceable contracts under the UCC. Matthew Zeidenberg purchased consumer copies of ProCD's SelectPhone software and resold the database commercially, in violation of the license terms. The district court ruled the license unenforceable; the Seventh Circuit reversed.

Issue

Is a shrinkwrap license a valid and enforceable contract when full terms are only disclosed after purchase?

Rule

Contract law governs the relationship between the parties; copyright law does not supersede contract law. A buyer who continues use after reviewing terms has accepted them.

Analysis

Zeidenberg had the opportunity to review and reject the terms by returning the software. His continued use constituted acceptance. Commercial use violated the personal-use license.

Conclusion

Shrinkwrap licenses are enforceable. ProCD was entitled to enforce the license. The circuit court reversed the district court and ruled in ProCD's favor.

Ethics Analysis

Ethical Hacking: Definitions, Hacker Types & Organizational Benefits

Analysis

This graduate-level analysis defined ethical hacking as the authorized use of offensive security techniques to identify and remediate vulnerabilities — distinguishing it from malicious activity solely by authorization and intent. The paper examined six hacker archetypes through the "hat color" framework and connected ethical hacking benefits to real-world organizational security practice.

A key argument was that the word "hacker" is not monolithic — motivations define the ethics. Black hat hackers exploit systems for personal gain or disruption; white hats are authorized professionals who strengthen security posture; gray hats operate without permission but usually cause no harm; red hats aggressively pursue black hats outside legal channels; blue hats are pre-release security testers; green hats are newcomers who may cause unintentional harm through inexperience.

The analysis concluded by connecting ethical hacking to two organizational benefits: proactive vulnerability identification before adversarial exploitation, and systemic improvement of both technical and human security practices.

Case Law — Copyright / Fair Use

FIRAC Brief: Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc. (U.S. Supreme Court 2021)

FIRAC Brief

Google v. Oracle is one of the most significant copyright cases in software history. Google copied 11,500 lines of Oracle's Java SE API to build Android, arguing the copying was necessary to attract Java developers to a new platform. Oracle sued for copyright infringement. The Supreme Court ruled 6-2 in Google's favor, finding the use constituted fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

Issue

Does Google's copying of Oracle's Java SE API for Android constitute fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107?

Rule

Fair use evaluates four factors: purpose/character of use, nature of the work, amount used, and market effect. All four must be weighed holistically.

Analysis

Google's use was transformative (new platform); the API is functional (limits protection); only 0.4% of the codebase was copied; Android does not substitute for Java SE in the market.

Conclusion

All four fair use factors favored Google. The Court ruled Google's limited, transformative copying of the Java SE API constituted fair use. Ruled in favor of Google.

Ethics & Policy Analysis

The Ethics of State-Sponsored Cyber Operations

Policy Paper

This policy paper examined the ethical dimensions of state-sponsored cyber operations — analyzing motivations, the offensive/defensive distinction, and the fairness of cyberwarfare as an instrument of national power. The central argument was that the ethics of a cyber operation depend heavily on its purpose, scope, and proportionality rather than the act of hacking itself.

Defensive cyber operations — protecting critical infrastructure, monitoring foreign threats, and neutralizing incoming attacks — were characterized as generally ethically justified, operating within a nation's legitimate security mandate. Offensive operations occupy more contested ethical ground, particularly when they affect civilian infrastructure, escalate diplomatic tensions, or produce collateral damage beyond the intended target (e.g., the NotPetya attack's global spread).

The paper concluded that cyberwarfare lacks the international norms, rules of engagement, and accountability mechanisms that govern kinetic warfare — making diplomacy, cooperative frameworks, and restraint in offensive operations essential to preventing escalation. Offensive operations should be reserved for when they are genuinely necessary and proportionate.

Takeaways

Broader Perspective

Across these assignments, a consistent theme emerges: cybersecurity decisions are rarely purely technical. Whether analyzing a shrinkwrap license, a fair use defense, the ethics of offensive hacking, or the design of a community education program — context, intent, and consequence shape what is legal, ethical, and effective.

This course reinforced that strong cybersecurity practitioners need more than technical skill. They need the ability to reason through ambiguity, communicate clearly across legal and policy domains, and understand the broader societal impact of the work they do. These capabilities translate directly into GRC roles, security leadership, policy advisory positions, and any environment where security intersects with law, compliance, or organizational strategy.
